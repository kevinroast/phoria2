Product shots

Carrying on from Tags and plots, this time it's the kind of picture you'd find on a product page - a model on a plinth in a studio, with soft shadows, glass and light transmission. At the end, refining an image good enough to print. As before, every step is a complete program running beside its code, which you can copy as one HTML file or open in CodePen. Allow about fifteen minutes.

Part 5: A product shot

Everything in this part is in the core library, apart from loading the model, which is phoria2/gltf. The extra pieces - glass, soft shadows, caustics - are only loaded when a scene uses them.

1. A model from the web

loadGLTF() loads a glTF model and gives you back a node to add to the scene. These sunglasses are one of Khronos's sample models, and they're compressed - Draco for the shape and WebP for the textures. You don't need to do anything about that: phoria2 fetches the decoder the first time a file needs it. The model is its real size in metres, so the plinth is too, 40cm across, and view.frame() fits the camera round it.

There are no lights yet. environment = 'studio' lights everything from a built-in studio, which also gives the lenses something to reflect - turn the view and watch their colours shift.

2. Studio light

A DirectionalLight works like the sun: its light all comes from the same direction, and with castShadow it throws shadows. The plinth needs receiveShadow to show them, and each part of the model needs castShadow - model.meshes lists them all. shadowSoftness keeps a shadow sharp where things touch and softens it further away, as it would be under a big studio light. Turning environmentIntensity down lets the light and its shadows stand out more.

The light comes from behind, so the shadows fall towards you. direction is the way the light travels, not where it comes from.

3. A glass ball

transmission: 1 makes a material glass, so light goes through it instead of bouncing off. thickness is how far the light travels inside it - for a solid ball, that's its whole width - and ior is how strongly it bends: 1.5 for glass, 1.33 for water. Look through the ball and you'll see the plinth upside down, like you would through a real solid one!

Clear glass only casts a faint shadow, darker round the edge where the light just skims it. That outline is what a real glass's shadow looks like too.

4. Light through glass

attenuationColor tints the glass from the inside, and attenuationDistance is how far light goes through it before it's turned that colour - so thicker parts are deeper in colour, and the shadow is amber as well. caustics: true on the light bends its light through the glass, which focuses it into that bright spot in the middle of the shadow, like a glass of whisky on a sunny table.

One light in a scene can have caustics. It needs castShadow, and so does the glass. Glass with a coloured texture - stained glass, say - throws its pattern of colours too.

5. Refining an image

refine: true keeps drawing once nothing is moving, and averages each picture into the last one, so shadows, glass and caustics get smoother over a second or two. As soon as you turn the view it goes back to the normal fast picture. Refined pictures get optional depth of field as well: the camera's aperture is the size of its lens, and anything nearer or further than the point it's looking at goes soft - here the ball and the front edge of the plinth.

6. Save an image

view.toImage() gives you the picture as a PNG. samples refines it first, whether or not the view is refining, and scale: 2 draws it at twice the canvas's size - enough for print. It waits for anything that's still loading, so you never save a half-drawn picture.

Try it with your own model. Put its address in loadGLTF(). If it isn't made in metres, frame the model with view.frame(model) instead of the plinth, and scale the plinth to suit.
